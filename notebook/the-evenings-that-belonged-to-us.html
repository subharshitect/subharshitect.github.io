The Evenings That Belonged to Us

Somewhere in Sodepur, Winter of 2019

There is a certain kind of waking up that I have never experienced again.

It is strange, because nothing particularly extraordinary happened on those evenings. There was no occasion, no celebration, no great adventure waiting for us. In fact, most of the time, we had absolutely nothing planned. And yet, of all the things I remember about those years, I remember waking up.

It was 2019, in Sodepur. Winter had either arrived or was just beginning to. I cannot remember exactly. But I remember how those evenings felt.

There was a mess, or a PG, whatever you would call it, where two of my friends, Sudip and Majhi, lived. It was an ordinary building with two floors and an open terrace above, without any railings. Sudip stayed downstairs, and Majhi upstairs. Most of our gatherings happened in Sudip's room.

It was a small room with two narrow beds. There was hardly enough space for all of us, but somehow, we always managed. Someone would sit on the floor, someone on the bed, someone would find a little corner and make himself comfortable. There were usually four of us, and almost always a fifth person who happened to join us that day.

I lived a little farther away, at Dum Dum Cantonment. Getting there involved an auto or a train, followed by a little walking. I made that trip to their mess so often that, after a while, I stopped feeling like a visitor. It was simply where I went.

On some days, we would leave college early. On others, we would bunk our classes altogether. We would gather in that little room in the afternoon, doing this and that, and on some days we'd begin drinking. By five or six, we would have had enough to put us all to sleep. And so we slept. All of us, scattered across those two beds and whatever little floor was left between them. I would call my mom and tell her that I was staying over for the night.

Then, sometime around eight or nine in the evening, we would wake up.

I remember that waking up more clearly than almost anything else.

The room would be dark. Outside, the afternoon would have quietly disappeared. There would be that strange taste in my mouth after hours of drinking and sleeping. Someone would still be asleep. Someone else would be looking for his phone. Majhi, who did not drink back then, would usually be the one waking us up. And slowly, one by one, we would come back to life.

There was something so deeply comforting about those moments. The day was over. There were no classes left to attend, no appointments, no obligations that demanded our presence. We had slept through an entire afternoon, and somehow, nothing important had been lost. In fact, something wonderful was about to begin. We still had the whole night ahead of us.

I think that is what made those moments so special. We had no idea what we were going to do, but there was never a shortage of possibilities.

We were young, we had very little money, and our minds still had so much empty space in them. There were worries, of course, but they had not yet learned to occupy every corner of our thoughts. We had time to think of foolish things. Time to invent plans that would lead nowhere. Time to laugh about something for half an hour, forget about it, and begin laughing again.

Oddly enough, through all those college days, we never once went to watch a film together in a theatre. Not once. Partly because we couldn't afford to spend money carelessly. But also because there was always something else we would rather do. We loved films. We watched plenty of them on our phones, because we didn't even have a proper arrangement to watch something together in the room. But when we were together, there was so much to talk about. Perhaps sitting silently in a theatre for two hours felt like giving away two hours that could have been ours. And those hours mattered, even though we didn't know it then.

We would step outside, usually wearing our jackets, because by then the first cold evenings had begun to settle over Sodepur. There was a particular pleasure in those first days of winter. The air felt different. The streets felt different. Even the ordinary act of stepping out after a long sleep had a little happiness in it.

Our first stop was almost always Dadu's shop. We would stand there, have some chop-muri or whatever snacks were available, light our cigarettes, and begin talking. Mostly nonsense, I suppose. There was Sudip, Soumik, Majhi, and me. And whoever else had joined us that evening. We would make fun of Sudip. We would argue, laugh, tease one another, and discuss the terribly important question of what we were going to do that night.

And from there, we would begin walking.

There was a little pond, with a narrow path running beside it. We would take that path, walk along the water, and find our way through the lanes towards the main road that connected to our college. I remember those walks. Not because of anything we saw, but because of how little we needed. A few friends, an evening, a road we had walked a hundred times. That was enough.

Across from our college was Sambhu Da's shop. We would often end up there, as though all our walks were somehow meant to lead us back to that place. Sometimes we would find a senior, sometimes an alumnus, sometimes another familiar face who would stop and talk to us. There were always people to meet. Always another conversation to begin.

We would sit across from each other at those little tables, eat an egg devil, smoke another cigarette, and wonder what we should have for dinner. I remember looking at my college from there. And feeling something I have struggled to find a word for ever since.

Fulfilment, perhaps.

Not the kind that comes from having achieved something. Not the satisfaction of knowing that your life is going exactly where you want it to. It was much simpler than that. I would look at those buildings, those roads, those familiar shops, and feel that I belonged there. That this was my second home. That I had my people. And for that particular evening, at least, everything was fine.

It wasn't that life had no problems. Of course it did. But none of them felt larger than the comfort of being there, among people with whom I never had to think about what to say next.

Sometimes we would walk further, towards the dental gate, where the dentistry students entered the campus. We would find ourselves standing beside the highway, looking around, talking, sometimes not talking at all. We could spend an astonishing amount of time simply standing somewhere. There was no destination we were desperate to reach.

Other evenings, our walks would take us towards the railway tracks behind the PG. There was a road that ran alongside them, leading towards Sodepur station. We would walk along that road, sometimes with a train moving parallel to us, sometimes with nothing but the tracks beside us. There were old men sitting together playing cards, people returning home, and students from our college taking that familiar route across the railway. Some were heading for the station, others towards the autos that left for Madhyamgram from the other side. It was an ordinary road, full of people going somewhere. And there we were, walking along it with nowhere in particular to go.

I remember a certain point where the road seemed to end and the railway station began. It was a familiar place for another reason, too. That was where I would sometimes wait for my girlfriend back then when she arrived by train, or walk with her to say goodbye when she was leaving. I had stood there so many times for so many different reasons. Sometimes waiting for someone I loved at the time, sometimes watching her leave, and on other evenings, passing through the very same place with my friends, laughing about something entirely unrelated.

I suppose that is how places become a part of us. They quietly gather little pieces of our lives without our noticing. A place to "drop someone off," or, without knowing it, to "say goodbye to someone for one last time." And years later, when we remember that road, we remember more than just the road. There are people who once belonged to those ordinary moments, whose presence has since become a faint, tender part of the memory. Not everything that belongs to the past needs to be mourned. Some things simply remain there, like a passing shadow, among the many other beautiful things that happened. Different people, different evenings, and somehow the same little stretch of road quietly holding all of them.

And sometimes we would go farther, crossing towards the other side of the railway, where the autos for Madhyamgram waited. There was a bridge, a number of little shops, people coming and going, and that familiar chaos of a place where so many journeys seemed to meet. We would hang around there for a while, particularly on evenings when we felt like being somewhere a little more alive. It was noisy and crowded, and there was something comforting about that too. Sometimes we wanted the quiet road beside the tracks. Sometimes we wanted the noise of a hundred strangers. We had the freedom to choose either, and all the time in the world to change our minds. We would walk from one end of that little world to the other, from where Sodepur seemed to begin to where it seemed to end, without ever really thinking of leaving it.

And somewhere along those walks, there was the liquor shop.

That was usually when Soumik and I would exchange a look.

I should say something about Soumik. He was the only one among us who had a vehicle, a white scooty. And he would come all the way from Rishra, sometimes bringing that scooty onto a boat to cross the river. I still find that image funny. A man and his white scooty, travelling across a river, just to spend another evening doing absolutely nothing with his friends. There was something rather beautiful about that effort, although I doubt any of us ever thought of it that way.

So, when we passed the liquor shop, I would look at Soumik. He would look at me. And one of us would ask, almost casually, "Shall we drink again tonight?" And that would be enough. Another plan was born.

We would walk a little farther, buy whatever we could afford, and pick up snacks along the way. Packets of chips, alur chop, anything that seemed like a good idea at the time. We would carry everything back to the room and begin all over again.

There was always something amusing about our little arrangements. We had no proper place to sit, no proper glasses sometimes, and certainly no particular talent for handling alcohol. I remember throwing up quite a lot. Soumik did too. Sudip, perhaps, on more than a few occasions. We drank more than we knew how to handle. There was always the possibility that the owner might suddenly turn up, and that thought would occasionally frighten us into behaving ourselves for a few minutes.

But the fear never lasted very long. There would be laughter again. Someone would say something ridiculous. Someone would want to go outside. We would find ourselves back on the highway, walking without any reason, and eventually return to that same small room.

And by the next morning, there would be a hangover.

The wonderful thing was that, back then, we could afford a hangover. We could wake up feeling terrible, complain about it, go to college, sit around, and somehow allow an entire day to pass without feeling guilty about having wasted it. Time was something we had in abundance. Or perhaps we only believed that we did.

I think about that now. How freely we spent our days. How carelessly we gave away our afternoons to sleep and our evenings to walking. How a small amount of money, divided among a few friends, could buy an entire night of happiness. And how little of that happiness depended on anything actually happening.

Even today, people who knew us back then sometimes ask about the others. "Are you all still in touch?" "Are you still friends?"

There is something strangely touching about those questions. As though people remember us not merely as individuals, but as a little group that always appeared together. As though our friendship had become part of how they remembered those years, too.

And yes, we are still friends.

Life has taken us through different places, different responsibilities, different versions of ourselves. We have grown older. We have learned things we didn't know then. Some things have become better, others more complicated.

Life has offered beautiful things since those days. I would never say that all the happiness of my life was left behind in Sodepur. But there was something in those evenings that I have never quite found again.

Perhaps it was the innocence of newly found friendship. Perhaps it was the freedom of having nothing urgent to accomplish. Perhaps it was the strange confidence that tomorrow would somehow take care of itself. Or perhaps it was simply the age we were. An age when we could spend an afternoon doing nothing, wake up at eight, and feel that the best part of the day was still waiting for us.

There was no need to make the evening memorable. We didn't take those walks thinking that one day we would miss them. We didn't sit at Sambhu Da's shop wondering if these were the happiest years of our lives. We never looked at one another and said, "Remember this."

Why would we?

We thought we would always be able to do it again. Another afternoon. Another drink. Another nap in Sudip's room. Another winter evening waiting outside.

That, I think, is the quiet sadness of growing older.

You don't always miss the extraordinary things. Sometimes you miss the most ordinary hour of an ordinary day, in a place you never imagined you would one day remember so tenderly.

And now, when I think about Sodepur, I don't first remember the college, the drinking, or the nights that went on for too long. I remember waking up. I remember opening my eyes in that dark, crowded room, with the stale taste of sleep still in my mouth. I remember hearing my friends talking. I remember knowing that it was eight in the evening, that winter had arrived, and that outside, the little streets of Sodepur were waiting for us.

Dadu's shop would still be open. The pond would still be there. Sambhu Da would probably be at his shop. Soumik's white scooty would be parked somewhere. And in that small room, everyone was still there. Everyone I needed for that evening.

I don't think we ever understood how much happiness there was in that simple certainty. We didn't need to. We only had to get up, put on our jackets, and step outside.

The night belonged to us.

And perhaps the most beautiful thing about those evenings was that nobody ever said so.

We just knew.

Or perhaps we didn't.

Perhaps we were only living, without knowing that someday, years later, one of us would sit somewhere far away and try to find the words for something as simple as waking up beside his friends.

We thought there would always be another evening.

And I think, more than anything else, that is what I miss.
