Kaleidoscopes of cooperation

Can cooperation survive without anyone choosing to be good?

Studies

The original

The Prisoner’s Dilemma rewards betrayal: whatever the other player does, you do better by defecting, yet two defectors end up worse off than two cooperators. Theory said cooperation should vanish. In 1992 Martin Nowak and Robert May placed players on a grid, gave them no memory and no strategy, nothing but “always cooperate” or “always defect”, and let them copy whichever neighbour had done best.

Cooperators and defectors persisted together indefinitely, in proportions that kept changing. Started from a symmetric arrangement, such as one defector in a sea of cooperators, the lattice unfolded into what they called dynamic fractals, which became known as evolutionary kaleidoscopes.

Martin A. Nowak and Robert M. May, “Evolutionary games and spatial chaos”, Nature 359, 826–829 (1992). doi:10.1038/359826a0

What the plate shows

A 99 × 99 lattice whose edges wrap around. Each round, every site plays its eight neighbours and itself: a cooperator earns 1 from each cooperator it meets, a defector earns b from each cooperator, and everything else earns 0. Then every site, all at once, takes the strategy of the best scorer among itself and its neighbours.

At b = 1.85 a single defector grows into a symmetric pattern that never repeats. The symmetry is exact: nothing in the rule can break it. From a random start (the Chaos from noise study) the share of cooperators settles near 32%, the value reported in the literature; the readout under the plate measures it as you watch. Below b = 9/8 defectors stay isolated and the lattice freezes; above 2, defectors take nearly everything.

What it does not show

Everyone updates at the same instant, the lattice is perfect, and no one makes mistakes. Later work showed these details matter: letting players update one at a time instead of all at once can change the outcome. The model shows that spatial structure can sustain cooperation; it does not show that it does so in any particular population.

Reflection

No one in this lattice is good. Nobody remembers a kindness or punishes a betrayal; each site only copies whoever nearby did best. Cooperation survives anyway, in clusters, because cooperators next to cooperators do well, and doing well is the only thing anyone imitates. Goodness here is not a virtue but a geometry: a matter of who your neighbours are.
