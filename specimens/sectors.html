Specimens · Plate VI · 2007

Genetic sectors

How much of what survives is merit, and how much is position?

Observe

Studies

The original

Oskar Hallatschek and colleagues mixed two strains of E. coli that were genetically identical except for the colour of a fluorescent protein, one cyan (CFP), one yellow (YFP). They placed a droplet of about a million cells on an agar plate and watched the colony grow for four days.

The well-mixed homeland at the centre stayed speckled. But from its edge outward the colony split into single-coloured flares, which coarsened into a few large sectors reaching the rim. Growth measurements showed neither strain had a significant advantage. The sectors were drift, not selection: only a thin band of pioneers at the edge, about 70 µm deep, passes its genes on, so chance acts on a small population even inside a very large one.

The boundaries between sectors wander faster than a simple random walk (they measured an exponent of 0.65 ± 0.05, close to the 2/3 of the Eden growth model), and in circular colonies the growing circumference eventually pulls them apart, so the number of surviving sectors reflects the size of the homeland rather than the number of founders.

Oskar Hallatschek, Pascal Hersen, Sharad Ramanathan and David R. Nelson, “Genetic drift at expanding frontiers promotes gene segregation”, PNAS 104, 19926–19930 (2007). doi:10.1073/pnas.0710150104

What the plate shows

An Eden model, the simple growth model the paper compares its colonies with. A circular homeland is filled with the two strains at random. Then, one cell at a time, a random empty site on the colony’s edge is filled by a copy of a random neighbour. Nothing favours either strain. The faint rings mark the edge at regular intervals, like the time-lapse lines in the paper’s figures.

Watch the speckle at the homeland’s edge sort itself into flares, and the flares lose to one another until a few sectors own the rim. The readout counts the sector boundaries crossing the edge. Try the 95 : 5 study: most of the rare strain is squeezed out early, but some of it surfs the front to the rim. A smaller homeland leaves fewer sectors.

What it does not show

Real colonies have nutrients, cell shapes, thickness and mechanical pushing; this is cells on a square lattice, which grows slightly faster along the grid’s axes. There is no selection and no mutation here, and the plate is far smaller than a real colony, so expect only a handful of sectors.

Reflection

In this dish nothing is better than anything else, and still one colour ends up owning most of the rim. Being at the edge at the right moment is the only advantage, and it compounds. When we look at who prevailed and read the story backwards, as merit, we may be reading position as virtue. History is written by those who happened to be at the front.
