Epochs of innovation

    Record

    The original

    Can a row of cells, each seeing only its six nearest neighbours, decide something about the whole row? Rajarshi Das, Melanie Mitchell and James Crutchfield set a cellular automaton a task: if more than half of its 149 cells start as 1, settle to all 1s; otherwise settle to all 0s. No cell can count the whole row. The obvious rule, local majority, fails completely: it freezes into stripes.

    Instead of designing a rule they evolved one. A genetic algorithm kept 100 candidate rules, tested them on 100 fresh random rows each generation, kept the best 20 and bred the rest from them by crossover and mutation, for 100 generations. Most runs found simple strategies: guess one answer every time (about 50% right), or grow large blocks of one colour (about 65%). But some runs discovered rules that compute with particles, boundaries between regular regions that travel, collide and annihilate, carrying information across the row. The best, called φpar, classifies 77.5% of unbiased random rows correctly.

    Rajarshi Das, Melanie Mitchell and James P. Crutchfield, “A genetic algorithm discovers particle-based computation in cellular automata”, in Parallel Problem Solving from Nature III, Lecture Notes in Computer Science 866, 344–353 (Springer, 1994). PDF
    James P. Crutchfield, Melanie Mitchell and Rajarshi Das, “The evolutionary design of collective computation in cellular automata” (1998). arXiv:adap-org/9809001

    What the plate shows

    The plate is a space-time diagram: one row of 149 cells, time running downward. In the filtered view, cells inside a regular region (all 0s, all 1s, or a checkerboard) are drawn quietly, and only the boundaries between regions, the particles, are bright. That is the computation, made visible.

    Evolve your own run with the paper’s settings and watch fitness climb, usually in sudden jumps, the “epochs of innovation” the authors describe; select an epoch to see what that rule does. Compare it with φpar, published in 1998, with the hand-designed GKL rule (Gács, Kurdyumov and Levin, 1978), and with local majority. “Measure” estimates the share of random rows each rule gets right; on this page φpar measures about 0.78 and GKL about 0.81.

    What it does not show

    No rule of this kind can solve the task perfectly; Land and Belew proved that in 1995. The genetic algorithm finds particle strategies only in some runs, so yours may stop at blocks. The φpar table here is the one printed in the 1998 paper and reproduces its reported performance; a second published rule could not be reproduced from the table as retrieved, and is left out.

    Reflection

    Nobody told evolution about particles. It was only rewarded for right answers, and in some runs it arrived at a design an engineer would recognise: signals that travel, meet, and decide. The rule does not know it is computing. Meaning, here, is something an observer reads into regularities that selection kept because they worked.
